Why 2026 Is a Good Time to Look at Freehold Property in Singapore

By Serene Lee, ERA Realty · Published 24 Sep 2026 · 6 min read

Every few years, someone tells me freehold is "always worth the premium," and someone else tells me it's "a waste of money for a 10-year hold." Both are half-right. The honest answer depends on your numbers — but 2026 does have a few things going for it if freehold is on your shortlist. Here's the real picture, gap and all.

The 10-Year Price Story: Has the Gap Actually Narrowed?

Quick disclosure first: there's no single official government index that tracks "freehold vs. leasehold" — URA's price index is split by property type and region, not tenure. Every number you see quoted (mine included) comes from private research houses crunching transaction data, so treat any single figure as indicative, not gospel.

With that caveat, here's what the data broadly shows.

The sticker-price premium hasn't disappeared — it's roughly where it was a decade ago. One widely-cited decade-long study puts the psf premium for freehold at about 19% in 2016 and 20% in 2024 — basically flat.

But the value gap has compressed. Over 2013–2025, 99-year leasehold condos appreciated an estimated 33–38%, versus 25–30% for comparable freehold — leasehold simply had more room to run. Rental yields told a similar story: newer leasehold developments have been posting 3.0–3.8%, against 2.6–2.9% for older freehold stock.

So which is it — flat or narrowing? Both, depending on what you're measuring. The price tag premium hasn't moved much. The returns gap has — largely because newer, well-located 99-year projects with MRT integration and stronger branding have been closing the quality gap that used to justify a bigger freehold premium, while ageing freehold stock hasn't kept pace on facilities or positioning.

Why 2026 Specifically

A few conditions line up this year:

  • Financing has gotten easier. SORA has eased to roughly 3.0–3.5%, down meaningfully from the peak, which softens monthly repayments even with today's LTV limits (75/70/60% for 1st/2nd/3rd private property).
  • The market has cooled to a steadier pace, not a falling one. HDB resale price growth slowed to 2.9% in 2025 and flattened in Q4 — a sign of a market finding its footing rather than one you need to time perfectly.
  • The rules now favour longer holds. SSD relief now only kicks in after 4 years (extended from 3 in July 2025), pushing the market toward buy-and-hold behaviour — which happens to be exactly the horizon where freehold's advantages matter most.
  • Freehold land is structurally shrinking. Nearly all Government Land Sales sites released today are leasehold. Every year, the supply of new freehold stock stays close to zero while leasehold inventory keeps growing — a scarcity trend that compounds regardless of what happens to prices in any single year.

None of this is a signal to rush. It's a signal that the entry conditions — financing cost, market pace, holding-period incentives — are more favourable than they've been in a few years, whether you buy this year or in three.

5 Reasons Freehold Can Be the Better Buy

  1. No lease decay, ever. CPF usage on a leasehold home gets scaled down once the remaining lease can't carry the youngest buyer to age 95, and is cut off entirely once the lease has under 20 years left. A freehold property never triggers that wall for any future buyer — your resale pool stays wide indefinitely.
  2. Cleaner en bloc economics. A freehold site doesn't require a lease top-up payment to the state before redevelopment, which typically makes it a more attractive — and simpler — acquisition target for developers.
  3. A genuine legacy asset. It passes down without an expiry date — a real consideration for anyone thinking about estate planning or holding across generations, not just the next 10 years.
  4. Structural scarcity that compounds. With GLS sites now almost entirely leasehold, the freehold share of Singapore's private housing stock only shrinks over time. Scarcity value is one of the few property arguments that doesn't depend on the next quarter's transaction data.
  5. It widens your eventual buyer pool at the top end. Older buyers close to the CPF/age lease-coverage cut-offs, and estate-minded buyers generally, are far more likely to consider a freehold unit — segments a decaying leasehold property gradually loses access to.

5 Reasons Leasehold Can Be the Smarter Buy

  1. You keep 8–20% of your capital in your pocket. That's real money you can put toward a bigger unit, a better location, or simply a smaller loan — the freehold premium is an opportunity cost, not just a price tag.
  2. Newer stock, better yields. Recent 99-year launches often come with integrated MRT access, newer facilities, and rental yields running 3.0–3.8% versus 2.6–2.9% on older freehold buildings — real cash flow, not just paper appreciation.
  3. The returns haven't actually lagged. Over 2013–2025, leasehold appreciation (33–38%) outpaced freehold (25–30%) in aggregate — a shorter lease hasn't meant a worse investment, at least not over the last decade.
  4. Lower absolute price eases every other constraint. Smaller loan quantum, lower ABSD/stamp duty cash outlay, and less pressure against the 55% TDSR cap — which the market itself now flags as the real binding constraint on most 2026 buyers, ahead of LTV.
  5. For most ownership timelines, lease decay is a non-event. If you're holding for 10–15 years, you're nowhere near the CPF or financing cliffs that only bite once a lease drops toward 60 years or below. You may be paying a premium to insure against a risk that never actually reaches your holding period.

So Which Should You Buy?

Neither answer is universally right — it depends on your holding period, how tight your budget already is against TDSR, and whether resale flexibility or legacy value matters more to you personally. If you're planning to hold for 5–10 years, the leasehold return data is hard to ignore. If you're thinking multi-decade or intergenerational, freehold's scarcity argument gets a lot stronger.
